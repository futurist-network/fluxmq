1. Speed (consumed msg/s, end to end). One node, group commit (fsync before every ack): 1 KiB 231k (1×1) / 320k (4×4), 100 B 427k / 632k. Against Kafka's default (page-cache acks) that is worse at 1 KiB (0.44× / 0.55×) and 100 B 1×1 (0.66×), 3.0× better at 100 B 4×4 (Kafka's consumers fall behind: 210k; its second repeat was client-bound and is excluded). Against Kafka with fsync per message (equal durability): 0.81× / 0.73× at 1 KiB, 0.63× at 100 B 1×1, on par at 100 B 4×4 (632k vs 638k). Three nodes RF=3: 1 KiB 217k / 207k, 100 B 622k / 328k: 2.6× Kafka default and 1.7× Kafka-fsync at 1 KiB 4×4 (Kafka's 3-node 100 B 4×4 rows are client-bound and excluded), 0.86× Kafka default at 1 KiB 1×1 (1.36× Kafka-fsync), on par at 100 B 1×1. RabbitMQ: 11–38k (1 node), 6–11k (3 nodes) — FluxMQ is 16–85× faster. On one node FluxMQ's producers outrun its consumers (they publish 1.7–2× what is consumed); the limit is the broker's 2 CPUs at 1 KiB and the consumer SDK at 100 B 1×1 (see Improvements). Throughput, equal durability, default vs default. After one quick fix measured at the end (deferred raw-run compaction, see After quick fixes): 1 node 1 KiB 277k / 476k and 100 B 1×1 545k — 1 KiB 4×4 then beats Kafka-fsync (1.09×) and reaches 0.82× Kafka default; 3 nodes 1 KiB 239k / 297k.
  2. Latency (end to end, 1 KiB, 4×4). One node p50 / p99 / p99.9: 1.5 / 3.6 / 19 ms at 1k msg/s, 1.7 / 3.5 / 9.5 ms at 10k, 2.4 / 8.2 / 18 ms at 50k — the best tail of all systems (Kafka default p99 12 / 16 / 22 ms, Kafka-fsync 40 / 44 / 57 ms, RabbitMQ 2.6 / 49 ms, 50k not reachable). Three nodes p50 / p99: 2.7 / 7.5 ms, 7.6 / 19 ms, 14 / 131 ms: p99 better than Kafka default at 1k and 10k (8.3 / 84 ms) and far better than Kafka-fsync (100 / 259 / 601 ms), p50 higher than Kafka default (2.5–2.8 ms) and p99 at 50k slightly worse (131 vs 110 ms) — FluxMQ fsyncs on every in-sync replica before the ack, Kafka's default does not. Latency.
  3. Reliability (acked-but-lost after a crash). FluxMQ in its default group commit lost 0 acknowledged messages in every case: kill -9 and a simulated power cut (dirty page cache dropped, verified by a canary on every crash), on one node, on the leader of a 3-node cluster and on all 3 nodes at once — 12 crashes, 1.16M acked ids checked. Kafka with its default flush lost 90,094 acked messages in 2 of 2 power cuts on one node and 119,178 in 2 of 2 power cuts of all 3 nodes (0 after kill -9, and 0 when only the leader lost power, since a replica survived) — FluxMQ is more reliable than Kafka as shipped. Kafka with fsync per message lost 0 (equal durability), and FluxMQ with fsync=os ‡ loses like Kafka's default (95,606 on one node, 104,374 when all 3 nodes lose power), which is why it is not the default. Reliability.
  4. Fault tolerance (a broker dies under 5k msg/s, 3 nodes). Kill -9 of the broker leading partition 0, kept down 30 s, live producers and consumers: FluxMQ resumed acks after 5.9 s (5.4–6.4 s; detection = node_timeout) and the consumers after the same 5.9 s; Kafka after 11.4 s (8.7–14.0 s) — about 2× faster failover. 0 client errors (both clients retry internally) and 0 acked lost on both. When the node comes back FluxMQ's publishing pauses for 38 ms vs Kafka's 178 ms, and a restarted FluxMQ node is ready in 0.1–0.5 s vs 11–22 s for Kafka (20–100× faster restart). RabbitMQ was not measured here (the crash tool has no AMQP client). Fault tolerance.
  5. Overload and slow consumers. Producers unthrottled for 60 s. 100 B, 1 node: FluxMQ consumers still get 606k msg/s (37 % of 1.65M published; v0.6: 36k) — 3× Kafka default (201k), on par with Kafka-fsync (628k, which publishes less). 3 nodes, 100 B: FluxMQ 324k consumed; Kafka's 3-node 100 B overload rows are client-bound (1.5k consumed; Kafka-fsync's window stretched), so no ratio is quoted. 1 KiB: FluxMQ consumers keep up (363k on 1 node, 213k on 3 nodes) because the broker's CPU caps the producers; Kafka keeps up at 616k on 1 node (FluxMQ 0.59×) but falls behind on 3 nodes (76k of 195k: FluxMQ 2.8×). The broker stayed responsive (no errors, latency runs between scenarios unaffected); messages removed by the size cap before a slow group read them are counted and warned, never silent (0 unexplained in every run after the fix below). Overload.
  6. Catch-up (read a backlog). 100 B, 10.7M messages (1 GiB): FluxMQ 1.35M msg/s (8.0 s; 3 nodes 1.45M, 7.4 s) vs Kafka 0.61–0.69M (17.7 s; 3 nodes 0.50M, 21.5 s) — about 2× faster. 1 KiB, 1M messages: FluxMQ 477k msg/s, 2.2 s; Kafka's read rate after its first message is higher (1.9M msg/s) but its consumer-group join takes longer, so the wall time is on par (2.7 s). RabbitMQ: 44k (100 MiB backlog). Catch-up.
  7. Resources. Idle: FluxMQ 0.02 cores / 28 MiB (1 node), 78 MiB for 3 nodes; Kafka 0.06 / 436 MiB, 1.4 GiB for 3 nodes; RabbitMQ 134 / 319 MiB — FluxMQ uses 15–18× less memory at rest. Under 1 KiB 4×4 load FluxMQ uses its whole 2-CPU limit: 155k messages per broker CPU-second vs Kafka's 310k (default) and 423k (fsync) — Kafka is 2–2.7× more CPU-efficient at 1 KiB (it serves consumers with sendfile; FluxMQ copies). Load memory: FluxMQ 631 MiB vs Kafka 1.47 GiB (2.3× less). Resources.
  8. Cost of TLS (FluxMQ only). 1 node: −47 % at 1 KiB 1×1 (122k vs 231k), −46 % at 4×4, −16 % / −17 % at 100 B; p99 at 1k/10k/50k 7.3 / 19 / 8.5 ms. 3 nodes (client + node mTLS): −40 % / −47 % at 1 KiB, −7 % / −13 % at 100 B, p99 47 / 114 / 133 ms. TLS runs on the broker's 2 CPUs, which 1 KiB already saturates. Kafka and RabbitMQ were not measured with TLS. TLS.
  9. Cost of features. lz4 (1 node): consumed +7 % at 1 KiB 4×4 and +8 % at 100 B 1×1, −3 % / −8 % elsewhere; producers get much faster (1.33M vs 354k msg/s published at 1 KiB 4×4) and broker CPU drops to 1.3 cores; latency p99 2.8–3.5 ms (best of all). The bench payload compresses very well; real ratios vary. Cumulative acks: −0 to −7 % throughput and worse latency tails (p99 27–33 ms at 10k–50k) in this harness — no benefit measured; individual acks stay the default and the recommendation. Features.
  10. Scaling 1 → 3 nodes. Here three nodes add replication (RF=3, fsync on every in-sync replica), not capacity: all three share one VM and one SSD. FluxMQ loses 6 % (1 KiB 1×1) to 35 % (1 KiB 4×4) and gains at 100 B 1×1 (+46 %, the 1-node run was client-bound); Kafka default loses 51 % (1×1) and 86 % (4×4, consumers fall behind) at 1 KiB, RabbitMQ 34–72 %. FluxMQ keeps the most of its 1-node throughput. Scaling.
  11. Verdict. Better than Kafka: latency tails on one node (p99 3.5–8 ms vs 12–22 ms) with fsync before every ack; 3-node throughput at 1 KiB 4×4 (2.6×) and at 100 B with 4 consumers; consumers under overload (100 B: 3× on 1 node; 1 KiB: 2.8× on 3 nodes); one-node 100 B 4×4 saturation (3×); catch-up at 100 B (2×); memory (2.3× less); zero acked loss after power cuts where Kafka's default loses 90–119k; failover 2× faster and restarts 20–100× faster. On par: 100 B 1×1 on 3 nodes; catch-up wall time at 1 KiB; 100 B 4×4 vs Kafka-fsync on one node. Worse: one-node saturation at 1 KiB (0.44–0.55× Kafka default, 0.73–0.81× Kafka-fsync) and 100 B 1×1 (0.66× / 0.63×) — an engineering gap, not a durability trade-off, because Kafka with fsync per message is also faster: the broker copies every byte to consumers (no sendfile) and runs out of its 2 CPUs, and a single FluxMQ consumer tops out at ~430k msg/s at 100 B in the SDK. Also worse: 3-node p50 latency (a durability trade-off: fsync on every replica before the ack) and the 1 KiB 1×1 3-node rate (0.86×). Found and fixed by this benchmark before publishing: a lagging consumer group could skip stored, undelivered messages when size retention overtook it (at-least-once violation, now covered by a regression test; all FluxMQ rows here are from the fixed build).
Method and limits. One MacBook Pro (M1 Pro, 8 cores, 32 GB), Docker Desktop VM (8 CPUs, 9.7 GB), every broker container limited to 2 CPUs / 2 GiB, the client to 4 CPUs (1 node) or 2 (3 nodes); three "nodes" share one VM and one SSD, so 3-node results show replication cost, not horizontal scaling. Same harness and settings as v0.6 (window 20 000, 16 MiB segments, 512 MiB/partition retention, 30 s windows), FluxMQ in its default group commit. To fit the time budget this set uses 2 saturation repeats (v0.6: 3 on one node), catch-up and overload only on the headline systems, the lz4 / cumulative rows on one node only, and 2 crash iterations per mode. RabbitMQ's catch-up backlog is 100 MiB (not 1 GiB). Kafka runs at 100 B 4×4 where the harness container was CPU-bound and its measurement window stretched (5 of 6 at 3 nodes, 1 of 2 at 1 node) are excluded from the medians and kept in the set.
